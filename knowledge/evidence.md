# Evidence Base

Last evidence review: 2026-08-23

This file stores the scientific evidence used by Odyssey Training System. It deliberately stops at **what the research supports**. Odyssey-specific prescriptions belong in `knowledge/coaching-system.md`.

## Evidence-strength convention

These are pragmatic labels, not substitute GRADE ratings.

- **High** — recent systematic review/meta-analysis with reasonably consistent findings and a population/context close to endurance training.
- **Moderate** — systematic review/meta-analysis with heterogeneity, limited trials, or some population mismatch; or multiple consistent peer-reviewed studies.
- **Low** — small/single studies, observational evidence, scientific/practice reviews, substantial population mismatch, or indirect inference.

A recurring limitation is important: research usually supports **intensity domains, work-duration ranges, recovery principles, specificity, and load management**, not one uniquely correct combination of repetitions, rest and pace.

## Evidence map

### 1. Training-intensity distribution

**Evidence**

- Systematic reviews in middle- and long-distance runners generally support keeping most **whole-program** training at low intensity, with a smaller amount of threshold and high-intensity work.
- A 2024 meta-analysis found polarized training produced a small advantage for VO2peak compared with other distributions, but no clear superiority for time-trial performance, time to exhaustion, or threshold speed/power.
- Highly trained/elite runners successfully use both pyramidal and polarized patterns, with emphasis changing across the season.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate**.

**Population / context:** trained through elite endurance runners across multi-week/seasonal programs.

**Limitations:** TID is a whole-program concept; classification methods differ; successful elite practice is often observational. It cannot be reduced to “what Tuesday should always be.”

Key sources: PMID **34749417**, **38717713**, **29182410**, **35418513**.

### 2. Threshold / tempo training

**Evidence**

- Threshold-zone continuous running and medium/long aerobic intervals are established components of successful distance-running practice.
- Current reviews do **not** establish threshold-dominant total training as superior to pyramidal or polarized approaches.
- A 2024 meta-analysis on anaerobic-threshold outcomes favored HIIT over moderate continuous training, but contained only three studies (72 athletes), with high heterogeneity and low certainty.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** that threshold work is useful; **Low** for a unique optimal threshold format.

**Population / context:** trained endurance runners/athletes.

**Limitations:** “threshold” is defined differently across studies; the literature does not directly prove that 20 min continuous, 3 × 8 min, 2 km reps, or any other single format is universally best.

Key sources: PMID **34749417**, **35418513**, **38842374**.

### 3. VO2max / aerobic-power intervals

**Evidence**

- A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis covering 86 articles and 239 HIIT protocols found **long work intervals (≥2 min)** generally accumulate more time at or near VO2max than very short work intervals (≤30 s), with intermediate-duration intervals between them.
- Greater cumulative work duration was positively related to accumulated time at ≥90–95% VO2max.
- A 2026 meta-analysis comparing HIIT with sprint interval training (SIT) found HIIT superior for VO2max and maximal aerobic power/velocity, although evidence quality was mostly low/very low except for MAP/MAV.
- A broader interval-programming meta-analysis found improvements depend on training status and dose, and did not identify a universal optimal prescription.

**Evidence strength:** **High** for multi-minute intervals being effective at accumulating a high oxygen-uptake stimulus; **Moderate** for translating acute time-near-VO2max directly into long-term race superiority.

**Population / context:** healthy adults through trained endurance athletes; some reviews mix running and cycling.

**Limitations:** time near VO2max is a mechanistic proxy; definitions and protocols vary; longer is not automatically better if mechanics or pace quality collapse.

Key sources: PMID **42237396**, **41740126**, **33826121**, plus programming reviews PMID **23539308** and **23832851**.

---

### 4. Interval duration

**Evidence**

- Work-bout duration materially changes the acute physiological response.
- In highly trained middle-distance runners, a 2025 crossover study found 4 × 3 min accumulated more time above 90% VO2max than 24 × 30 s in the protocols tested.
- Meta-analytic evidence also shows HIIT time-trial response can depend on work-bout duration.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate-to-High** that interval duration is a meaningful programming variable; **Low** for one universally optimal duration.

**Population / context:** trained middle-distance/endurance athletes plus broader intervention datasets.

**Limitations:** protocol-specific; acute physiology is not identical to chronic adaptation.

Key sources: PMID **39835194**, **42237396**, **33826121**.

---

### 5. Recovery duration and active vs passive recovery

**Evidence**

- A 2024 systematic review found both active and passive recovery can support long-term interval-training adaptations and did not establish a consistent advantage for either.
- The 2026 time-near-VO2max meta-analysis found no meaningful overall active-versus-passive recovery effect on time near VO2max.
- In 11 well-trained male runners performing 4 × 2 min at 100% maximal aerobic speed, active and passive 2-min recoveries produced similar time above 90% VO2max, while passive recovery lowered perceived load.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** that neither recovery mode is universally superior; **Low** for transferring one exact recovery prescription across workout types.

**Population / context:** healthy/trained participants; runner-specific acute evidence includes trained men.

**Limitations:** recovery interacts with work duration, intensity, total session design and desired repeat quality.

Key sources: PMID **38443585**, **35262762**, **42237396**.

### 6. Speed endurance / sprint stimulus

**Evidence**

- Sprint interval training improves physical-performance outcomes, especially anaerobic outcomes, but published protocols are highly heterogeneous and meta-analyses report possible small-study effects.
- Running repeated-sprint training can improve sprint speed, repeated-sprint ability, VO2max and other qualities in trained athletes.
- For aerobic-capacity outcomes, current comparative evidence favors HIIT over SIT; sprint work therefore should not be treated as a substitute for aerobic-power training when aerobic power is the primary target.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** for speed/anaerobic adaptation; **Low-to-Moderate** for exact transfer to heterogeneous distance runners.

**Population / context:** healthy adults and trained athletes; substantial repeated-sprint evidence comes from team sports.

**Limitations:** SIT, repeated-sprint training, relaxed strides and maximal-velocity sprinting are different interventions and must not be conflated.

Key sources: PMID **36165995**, **38041768**, **41740126**, **42477971**.

### 7. Running economy

**Evidence**

- A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of 31 studies and 652 middle-/long-distance runners found high-load strength training and combined strength methods improve running economy; plyometrics can also improve economy in some testing conditions.
- A running-focused meta-analysis published in the 2026 volume of *Journal of Human Kinetics* found HIIT improved running economy versus moderate continuous training, with substantial heterogeneity in the underlying studies.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate-to-High** for high-load/combined strength improving running economy; **Moderate** for HIIT-related running-economy effects.

**Population / context:** middle-/long-distance and endurance runners.

**Limitations:** multi-week strength/plyometric interventions cannot be equated to a few drills or strides inside one Tuesday session.

Key sources: PMID **38165636**, **41766811**.

---

### 8. Neuromuscular / short-speed stimulus

**Evidence**

- Strength/plyometric evidence supports the trainability of neuromuscular qualities relevant to running economy.
- Sprint-training evidence supports development of speed and anaerobic qualities.
- These findings support including neuromuscular/speed stimuli as one tool, but do not prove that a specific drill sequence, stride count or sprint distance is required for distance runners.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** for the broad training qualities; **Low** for an exact running-only Tuesday prescription.

**Population / context:** runners plus trained athletes from broader sport populations.

**Limitations:** intervention type and population mismatch are substantial when converting strength/plyometric or team-sport sprint evidence to a club running session.

Key sources: PMID **38165636**, **36165995**, **38041768**.

---

### 9. Hill training

**Evidence**

- Uphill intervals can create substantial aerobic and neuromuscular overload.
- In a 2023 crossover study of well-trained runners, 4 × 5 min at 8% uphill produced more time at high oxygen uptake than a near-flat matched protocol with similar HR, lactate and RPE.
- A six-week randomized study in well-trained runners found multiple uphill interval formats improved 5-km performance on average, without identifying one universally best hill format.
- A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis found promising individual-study effects from uphill running but no significant pooled effects for several strength/jump/aerobic-capacity outcomes; certainty was low because studies were small and heterogeneous.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** that hills are a useful overload tool; **Low** for a single optimal hill prescription.

**Population / context:** well-trained runners in key primary studies; broader athlete/recreational samples in the 2026 review.

**Limitations:** grade, duration, speed, surface and athlete profile change the stimulus considerably.

Key sources: PMID **23538293**, **36875023**, **42536523**.

### 10. Warm-up / dynamic preparation

**Evidence**

- A systematic review/meta-analysis found adequate warm-up improved the large majority of included performance criteria.
- Prolonged static stretching immediately before explosive work can impair sprint/jump performance in some contexts; runner-focused evidence does not show a running-economy benefit from static stretching.
- A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found no significant acute effect of static, dynamic or PNF stretching on running economy and rated certainty low.
- Dynamic/neuromuscular preparation has supporting evidence in broader athletic populations, but one exact runner warm-up sequence is not established.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** that warming up before quality work is beneficial; **Moderate** against relying on prolonged static stretching immediately before explosive work; **Low** for one universal warm-up routine.

**Population / context:** athletes across sports, with some runner-specific studies.

**Limitations:** “warm-up” interventions vary substantially; conventional drills are not automatically evidence-based simply because they are common.

Key sources: PMID **19996770**, **35096248**, **40442558**.

### 11. 800–1500 m and ~1 km performance demands

**Evidence**

- In highly trained runners, a classic study estimated aerobic contribution at ~66% for 800 m and ~84% for 1500 m, illustrating substantial contributions from both aerobic and anaerobic systems.
- A scientific/practice review of world-class 800/1500 m runners shows diverse athlete profiles: 1500 m preparation is generally more aerobic, while 800 m preparation places greater emphasis on strength/power and lactate-tolerance qualities.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** for the mixed aerobic/anaerobic nature of 800–1500 m; **Low** for an exact 1000 m prescription.

**Population / context:** highly trained/world-class middle-distance runners.

**Limitations:** the 2001 energy-system study was small; 1000 m sits between 800 and 1500 m but cannot simply be assigned an arithmetic midpoint of their energy-system percentages.

Key sources: PMID **11194103**, **34021488**.

### 12. Marathon-oriented quality sessions

**Evidence**

- Reviews integrating world-class practice and science show marathoners retain threshold/higher-intensity work while increasing **race-pace-specific running** as the target marathon approaches.
- A 2025 analysis of training before **151,813 marathon performances by 119,452 runners** found the fastest groups accumulated much greater volume primarily through more low-intensity running; pyramidal distributions were most common among the fastest runners. This is observational and cannot prove causality.
- A meta-analysis/meta-regression of 85 studies found multiple training-volume/frequency/long-run variables statistically associated with faster marathon performance.
- A 2024 analysis of 92 published sub-elite plans found predominantly pyramidal structures but explicitly noted that describing recommended plans is not evidence that those plans are effective.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** for the broad role of race-specific work inside a larger marathon program; **Low** for an exact one-hour group-session prescription.

**Population / context:** world-class, competitive, recreational and sub-elite marathoners.

**Limitations:** much marathon evidence is observational/practice-derived; total weekly volume and long-run findings cannot be reproduced by one Tuesday session.

Key sources: PMID **35362850**, **39616560**, **31704026**, **38695978**.

### 13. Periodization / progression

**Evidence**

- Distance-running reviews describe successful programs as changing emphasis across preparation and competition phases rather than repeating one session type indefinitely.
- World-class practice reviews report that race-pace volume increases as the primary competition approaches.
- A block-periodization systematic review/meta-analysis found small favorable effects versus traditional periodization for VO2max and maximal power, but underlying studies were small and generally low quality.
- Direct evidence identifying one uniquely superior distance-running periodization model remains limited.

**Evidence strength:** **Moderate** for progression and increasing specificity toward competition; **Low-to-Moderate** for superiority of any named periodization model.

**Population / context:** trained/elite distance runners and mixed endurance-sport athletes.

**Limitations:** periodization studies evaluate whole programs; race calendars and total training loads vary substantially.

Key sources: PMID **29182410**, **35418513**, **35362850**, **31802956**.

---

### 14. Pre-race taper / sharpening

**Evidence**

- A 2007 meta-analysis found endurance performance tends to improve when volume is substantially reduced while intensity and frequency are largely maintained; approximately two weeks performed well across the pooled evidence.
- A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis similarly found tapering improves time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance and supports reduced volume while maintaining intensity/frequency, with successful tapers generally no longer than about three weeks.

**Evidence strength:** **High** for reducing total training load while retaining some intensity before important endurance competition; **Low** for one exact workout four days before a 1 km TT.

**Population / context:** competitive endurance athletes across multiple sports/event durations.

**Limitations:** taper research evaluates multi-day/week training programs, not a single club session at a fixed four-day offset from a short race.

Key sources: PMID **17762369**; Wang Z et al., PLoS One 2023, DOI **10.1371/journal.pone.0282838**.

## Research gaps relevant to Odyssey

1. **Mixed-ability group prescription:** little direct research tests one-hour community sessions spanning very different running levels.
2. **Time-based vs distance-based intervals:** both are widely used, but direct evidence that one is superior for heterogeneous groups is lacking.
3. **Exact pace bands:** evidence supports relative intensity and physiological domains more strongly than universal pace tables.
4. **Exact warm-up architecture:** warm-up is supported, but one exact combination of easy running, drills and strides is not.
5. **1 km TT sharpening:** 800/1500 physiology and taper evidence inform the problem, but exact 1000 m prescriptions remain an applied coaching decision.
6. **One-session-per-week ownership:** most studies evaluate complete programs with several weekly sessions; Odyssey controls only Tuesday, so whole-program findings must be applied conservatively.
