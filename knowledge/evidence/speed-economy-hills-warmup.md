## SPD-001 — Sprint and repeated-sprint training develop speed/anaerobic qualities, with limited directness to distance running

### Research question

What broad adaptations are supported by sprint-oriented training?

### Supported claim

Systematic reviews show sprint-interval and repeated-sprint training can improve sprint speed, repeated-sprint ability, anaerobic outcomes, and in some populations VO2max or intermittent-running performance. Protocols are highly heterogeneous, and repeated-sprint evidence includes substantial team-sport populations.

### Training target

- maximal/near-maximal speed qualities;
- rapid force production and movement frequency;
- ability to repeat fast efforts;
- anaerobic contribution and speed under fatigue, depending on protocol.

### Expected transfer

Potentially greater speed reserve and improved ability to access/maintain fast running. For a 1-km event, speed is useful but must be integrated with aerobic power and race-specific endurance.

### Acute evidence

Short maximal work creates rapid performance decline and high neuromuscular/anaerobic demand. Recovery requirements differ from aerobic-power work.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Sprint/repeated-sprint programs improve several speed and fitness outcomes. More repetitions are not automatically associated with greater VO2max improvement.

### Performance evidence

Evidence is strongest for sprint/repeated-sprint tests and intermittent sport. Direct race-performance evidence in heterogeneous distance runners is limited.

### Programming variables

- maximal versus submaximal speed;
- rep duration/distance;
- acceleration versus flying/max-speed exposure;
- recovery completeness;
- total sprint volume;
- mechanics and surface;
- intervention frequency.

### Population / context

- SIT review: healthy adults, heterogeneous protocols.
- Repeated-sprint meta-analysis: 40 publications and 541 trained athletes in intervention groups, many from team sports.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate** for speed/anaerobic adaptation.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Low-to-Moderate** for exact distance-running prescription.

### Limitations

Formal SIT, repeated sprinting, relaxed strides, and controlled speed endurance must not be merged into one category.

### What this does not prove

- that maximal sprinting is required weekly;
- that fast 200-600 m reps are sprint training;
- that sprint work substitutes for aerobic-power training;
- that more sprint volume is better.

### Sources

`PMID-36165995`, `PMID-38041768`, `PMID-42477971`, `PMID-41740126`

## ECO-001 — Running economy is trainable, but the strongest evidence comes from multi-week strength programs

### Research question

Which interventions improve the energetic cost of running?

### Supported claim

A 2024 meta-analysis of 31 studies and 652 runners found high-load and combined strength methods improved running economy, with some benefits from plyometrics depending on testing speed. A broader systematic review found running economy and 1.5-10 km TT performance often improved after ≥4-week strength programs, while VO2max was usually unchanged.

### Training target

Improve force production, musculotendinous function, and the energy cost of running through appropriately dosed multi-week strength/plyometric training.

### Expected transfer

A runner may use less energy/oxygen at a given submaximal speed and improve performance without necessarily increasing VO2max.

### Acute evidence

A few drills or strides can change readiness and movement, but they are not equivalent to the interventions in the economy meta-analyses.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Moderate evidence supports high-load, combined, and some plyometric programs over 6-24 weeks for economy. Results vary by method, athlete, and testing speed.

### Performance evidence

TT and sprint outcomes often improve alongside economy, but not in every study.

### Programming variables

- strength method/load;
- plyometric type and volume;
- intervention duration/frequency;
- concurrent running load;
- test speed;
- athlete strength/training status.

### Population / context

Middle- and long-distance runners from moderately trained to highly trained; controlled interventions generally ≥4-6 weeks.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate-to-High**.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Low** for claiming one Tuesday running workout or drill sequence improves economy; **Moderate** as background for speed/hill/strength rationale.

### Limitations

Odyssey does not currently manage a multi-week strength program. Economy is affected by many biomechanical and metabolic variables.

### What this does not prove

- that dynamic drills improve running economy chronically;
- that short strides reproduce high-load strength training;
- that economy improvement is guaranteed in every runner;
- that VO2max must improve for performance to improve.

### Sources

`PMID-38165636`, `PMID-29249083`, `PMID-41766811`

## NM-001 — Short-speed/neuromuscular running is a useful inference, not a directly proven drill formula

### Research question

Does evidence support small doses of fast running for distance runners?

### Supported claim

Sprint and strength/plyometric research establishes that speed, force, and economy-related neuromuscular qualities are trainable. This supports the use of high-quality short fast running as one tool, but no evidence establishes a required stride count, drill list, or distance for a mixed-ability Tuesday group.

### Training target

Expose runners to faster mechanics and cadence with low enough fatigue that form and speed remain high quality.

### Expected transfer

Potential improvement in speed reserve, comfort at faster rhythms, and readiness for race-specific work.

### Acute evidence

Progressive accelerations can prepare the athlete for faster running. Maximal efforts create a different and larger neuromuscular demand.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Direct evidence for Odyssey-style strides alone is limited. Stronger evidence concerns structured sprint, strength, or plyometric programs.

### Performance evidence

Indirect through sprint/strength studies and middle-distance practice.

### Programming variables

- acceleration length;
- target speed versus all-out;
- recovery completeness;
- repetitions;
- surface and footwear;
- technique deterioration;
- race proximity.

### Population / context

Indirect evidence from runners and trained athletes across sports.

### Evidence strength

**Low-to-Moderate**.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Moderate**.

### Limitations

The expected effect depends on whether the work is relaxed, near-maximal, maximal, uphill, or under fatigue.

### What this does not prove

- that A-skips or any drill are mandatory;
- that more strides are better;
- that short-speed work must be exhausting;
- that every runner should use the same pace or group.

### Sources

`PMID-29249083`, `PMID-38165636`, `PMID-36165995`, `PMID-34021488`

## HILL-001 — Uphill intervals are an overload tool; no single hill format is optimal

### Research question

What does uphill running add to interval training?

### Supported claim

Uphill running can increase aerobic and neuromuscular demand. In 17 well-trained runners, 4 × 5 minutes at 8% grade produced more high-oxygen-uptake time than a near-flat protocol with similar HR, lactate, and RPE. A six-week study in 20 well-trained runners found several hill formats improved 5-km performance on average without identifying one best format. A 2026 meta-analysis found low-certainty and inconsistent pooled effects across small heterogeneous studies.

### Training target

Depending on format:

- aerobic overload at reduced absolute speed;
- force-oriented running and uphill mechanics;
- short power/acceleration;
- speed endurance with lower horizontal speed.

### Expected transfer

Potential development of high aerobic demand, leg force/power, and performance. Transfer to flat running depends on protocol and athlete.

### Acute evidence

Grade increases energetic and muscular demand and changes mechanics. Long uphill intervals can increase time at high oxygen uptake.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Hill programs can improve performance/economy outcomes, but pooled certainty is limited and no optimal grade/duration is established.

### Performance evidence

The 2013 randomized study reported average 5-km improvement across hill groups; small samples prevent precise protocol ranking.

### Programming variables

- grade;
- rep duration/distance;
- running speed and effort;
- downhill/walk-back recovery;
- surface and footing;
- technique;
- calf/Achilles and muscular loading;
- total repetitions.

### Population / context

Well-trained runners in key primary studies; athlete/recreational populations in the broader meta-analysis.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate** for hills as a useful overload tool; **Low** for exact prescription.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** for Yoyogi hill use, with operational and safety constraints.

### Limitations

A 100-m hill dash and 5-minute uphill HIIT are different workouts. Downhill recovery also changes loading and time.

### What this does not prove

- that hills are safer or lower impact for every runner;
- that hill work must be maximal;
- that 15 repetitions are optimal;
- that hills should appear every month.

### Sources

`PMID-23538293`, `PMID-36875023`, `PMID-42536523`

## WU-001 — Warm-up is supported; one exact routine is not

### Research question

Does warming up improve subsequent quality-session performance?

### Supported claim

A broad systematic review/meta-analysis found warm-up improved most evaluated performance criteria. Dynamic warm-up evidence supports sprint/jump readiness in broader athletic populations. Runner-specific reviews are smaller and heterogeneous. The evidence supports progressive preparation, not one mandatory routine.

### Training target

Transition from low intensity to workout-specific speed and movement demand before the first hard repetition.

### Expected transfer

Better immediate readiness, reduced first-rep shock, and improved ability to express the intended pace/technique.

### Acute evidence

Warm-up can improve subsequent performance. Dynamic preparation is especially relevant before faster/explosive work.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Warm-up is an acute preparation strategy; chronic adaptation is not the primary claim.

### Performance evidence

Broad performance-test evidence supports warming up, but exact running-race benefits and routines vary.

### Programming variables

- easy-running duration;
- dynamic preparation duration;
- strides/accelerations;
- workout speed;
- temperature;
- transition time before the main set;
- runner familiarity.

### Population / context

Broad athletic populations, plus a small runner-focused evidence base.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate** for warming up; **Low** for a universal sequence.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** for the principle; **Moderate** for exact minutes/drills.

### Limitations

“Warm-up” is a bundle of interventions. More volume can create fatigue, especially inside a 60-minute session.

### What this does not prove

- that every session needs the same drills;
- that threshold day requires maximal-speed preparation;
- that a long warm-up is always better;
- that drills should be added for appearance.

### Sources

`PMID-19996770`, `PMID-37644585`, `PMID-35096248`

## WU-002 — Stretching claims require nuance

### Research question

Should static or dynamic stretching be treated as inherently beneficial or harmful before running?

### Supported claim

A 2025 running-economy meta-analysis of 15 acute studies and 181 participants found no significant acute effect of static, dynamic, or PNF stretching on running economy, with low certainty and heterogeneous protocols. Broader explosive-performance evidence suggests prolonged static stretching alone can impair sprint/jump performance, while dynamic warm-up often performs better. Runner-specific evidence is mixed.

### Training target

Use dynamic preparation when it improves readiness for the workout. Use static stretching only for a clear individual purpose and avoid prolonged static-only routines immediately before fast/explosive work.

### Expected transfer

Preserves readiness without making unsupported claims that stretching prevents injury, removes lactate, or automatically improves economy.

### Acute evidence

No clear pooled running-economy effect. Sprint/jump outcomes can be duration- and protocol-sensitive.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Too few running-economy studies for confident chronic conclusions.

### Performance evidence

Mixed and context-dependent.

### Programming variables

- stretching mode;
- duration per muscle;
- intensity;
- whether followed by dynamic activity;
- outcome: economy versus sprint/jump;
- athlete-specific restriction or discomfort.

### Population / context

Small, heterogeneous healthy/running samples; broader athletic warm-up studies.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate** against simplistic universal claims; **Low** for one best stretching routine.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Moderate**.

### Limitations

Running economy is not the only warm-up outcome. Static stretching of short duration within a mixed warm-up is not the same as prolonged static stretching alone.

### What this does not prove

- that static stretching is always harmful;
- that dynamic stretching automatically improves running economy;
- that stretching prevents injury in this setting;
- that everyone needs flexibility work before intervals.

### Sources

`PMID-40442558`, `PMID-35096248`, `PMID-37644585`

## Workout-family distinction

| Tool | Primary intended use | Avoid conflating with |
|---|---|---|
| Progressive strides | preparation / controlled fast mechanics | maximal sprint training |
| Short speed | high-quality fast running with generous recovery | exhausting SIT |
| Speed endurance | sustaining fast rhythm under accumulating fatigue | pure maximal velocity |
| Long hills | aerobic + muscular overload | 100-m hill dash |
| Short hills | acceleration/force/power depending effort | threshold training |
| Dynamic preparation | acute readiness | chronic strength/plyometric adaptation |
| Strength/plyometric program | multi-week economy/force development | a few minutes of drills |

Exact Odyssey formats remain coaching judgments informed by this distinction.
