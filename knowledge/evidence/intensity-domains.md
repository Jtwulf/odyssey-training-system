# Running Intensity Domains and Field Translation

Last reviewed: 2026-08-23

This file provides the common intensity language used by Odyssey. It does not create a permanent pace table.

---

## Practical map

| Domain / marker | Broad physiological meaning | Field interpretation | Important caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below first threshold | Moderate domain; relatively stable metabolic response | Easy/conversational running; low session strain | Talk test and RPE are useful but not laboratory-equivalent measurements |
| First threshold (`LT1`, `VT1`, related markers) | Approximate moderate-heavy boundary | Upper easy / steady transition | Different methods can produce different thresholds |
| Heavy domain | Above first threshold but below the heavy-severe boundary; a delayed steady state can be possible | Controlled steady, tempo, and lower-threshold work depending on athlete and definition | “Tempo” is not one universal physiological point |
| Second-threshold family (`LT2`, `VT2`, MLSS estimates) | Methods intended to identify an upper sustainable boundary | Often used to anchor threshold training | These methods are related but not interchangeable |
| Critical speed (`CS`) | Running estimate of the heavy-severe boundary / maximal metabolic steady state | Useful anchor for training and performance modelling | Testing protocol and mathematical model affect the estimate |
| Severe domain | Above CS; finite work/distance capacity above CS is progressively depleted | Aerobic-power intervals and many 3–5 km/shorter race demands | The same pace can have different relative meaning across runners |
| `vVO2max` / maximal aerobic speed (`MAS`/`MAV`) | Speed associated with maximal oxygen uptake in a specific test | Useful prescription reference for high-intensity intervals | It is test-dependent and is not the same as CS or a race pace |
| Extreme / sprint domain | Exhaustion can occur before VO2 reaches a steady maximum; large neuromuscular/anaerobic demand | Very short speed, maximal sprint, some short hill work | Must not be conflated with relaxed strides or controlled speed endurance |

The map is conceptual. Individual testing, recent performance, environment, and workout type determine the practical prescription.

## DOM-001 — Running intensity is better represented by domains than by universal pace labels

### Research question

What framework best explains why the physiological response changes non-linearly as running speed rises?

### Supported claim

Running intensity can be organized into moderate, heavy, severe, and extreme domains separated by physiological boundaries. Critical speed is supported as an estimate of the heavy-severe boundary. The first threshold marks the lower moderate-heavy transition, although its exact relationship to CS varies between people and methods.

### Training target

Use domains to identify the primary stress of a session before selecting pace, duration, distance, and recovery.

### Expected transfer

A domain framework improves consistency between workout purpose and prescription. It helps prevent a label such as “tempo,” “threshold,” or “VO2” from being treated as a complete workout definition.

### Acute evidence

Above the heavy-severe boundary, physiological variables do not stabilize in the same way as below it, and the finite capacity above CS is progressively depleted.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Not directly tested by the domain classification itself. Training interventions at different intensities can improve different performance determinants, but domain terminology does not prove one optimal distribution.

### Performance evidence

CS and the balance between CS and D' are strongly related to middle- and long-distance running performance in the reviewed literature.

### Programming variables

- speed relative to individual boundaries;
- work duration and cumulative duration;
- recovery and incomplete restoration of D';
- test modality and surface;
- temperature, gradient, and fatigue state.

### Population / context

- Scoping review: 124 running studies covering measurement, physiology, performance, and training application.
- Systematic review: 26 studies and 527 participants examining the first threshold relative to CS.

### Evidence strength

**High** for CS as a useful heavy-severe boundary concept; **Moderate** for field translation of the lower boundary.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** for running concepts; **Moderate** for assigning exact paces without individual testing.

### Limitations

Different protocols and models produce different CS/D' estimates. First-threshold estimates show meaningful between-study and individual uncertainty.

### What this does not prove

- that every runner must undergo laboratory testing;
- that one pace percentage is correct for everyone;
- that `LT2`, `VT2`, `MLSS`, and `CS` are numerically identical;
- that a workout above CS is automatically a VO2max workout.

### Sources

`PMID-41931241`, `PMID-39048094`

## DOM-002 — Threshold methods are related, not interchangeable

### Research question

Can threshold labels obtained from lactate, ventilation, heart-rate variability, or field performance be substituted without qualification?

### Supported claim

Methods intended to identify lower or upper intensity boundaries often show group-level proximity, but individual limits of agreement can be wide. Terminology and protocols vary, and the resulting speed or heart-rate anchors should not be treated as interchangeable measurements.

### Training target

Use the most relevant and repeatable marker available, while preserving uncertainty and calibrating against recent running performance.

### Expected transfer

This reduces false precision in pace groups and prevents a single threshold estimate from becoming a permanent runner identity.

### Acute evidence

Threshold comparisons are primarily measurement studies. They show that different physiological signals may change near similar transitions but can yield different individual values.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Not directly tested.

### Performance evidence

Threshold-related speeds are associated with endurance performance, but measurement agreement is the relevant issue here.

### Programming variables

- test stage length;
- treadmill versus track/road;
- lactate method and curve definition;
- ventilatory method;
- heart-rate lag and environmental drift;
- athlete familiarity and pacing.

### Population / context

The HRV-threshold review included 27 studies and 461 healthy participants and noted heterogeneous methods and limited female representation.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate**.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Moderate**: the warning against interchangeable labels is directly relevant, but Odyssey normally lacks laboratory thresholds.

### Limitations

The evidence is measurement-focused and does not establish the best practical threshold tool for a heterogeneous community group.

### What this does not prove

- that threshold testing is useless;
- that RPE or breathing cues are equivalent to a laboratory threshold;
- that a fixed heart rate accurately preserves intensity in heat or accumulated fatigue.

### Sources

`PMID-37462761`, `PMID-41931241`, `PMID-40134905`

## DOM-003 — Critical speed can inform prescription, but testing quality matters

### Research question

Can CS be estimated in the field and used to individualize running training?

### Supported claim

Field-based time-trial and 3-minute all-out approaches can provide practical CS estimates when test conditions and modelling requirements are followed. CS/D' modelling has been used to individualize training and predict performance, but estimates are sensitive to trial duration, number of trials, model choice, pacing, and fatigue.

### Training target

Use CS as one possible organizing anchor for controlled threshold/severe-domain work, not as an obligatory database field for every Odyssey participant.

### Expected transfer

Where reliable recent race or time-trial data exist, CS concepts can improve the selection of relative work intensity and expected sustainable duration.

### Acute evidence

Field-testing studies primarily assess validity and reliability rather than workout response.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

The scoping review identified training applications, but the protocols are heterogeneous and do not establish one superior CS-based program.

### Performance evidence

CS and D' are strong determinants across mile-to-marathon and 5,000/10,000 m contexts in the reviewed running literature.

### Programming variables

- trial duration spread;
- at least two/usually more maximal efforts depending on model;
- surface, wind, and temperature;
- mathematical model;
- whether D' estimation is sufficiently reliable;
- current versus stale performance data.

### Population / context

- 19 field-testing studies in the runner-specific systematic review.
- 124 studies in the broader running scoping review.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate-to-High** for CS measurement/application; **Low** for a single practical protocol in Odyssey.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Moderate**: relevant to running, but repeated maximal testing and model maintenance are outside current scope.

### Limitations

Odyssey does not manage all runner testing or whole-program training. CS should be used opportunistically when credible data exist.

### What this does not prove

- that CS must be calculated before every session;
- that D' is stable across conditions and training phases;
- that estimated CS from one casual run is reliable.

### Sources

`PMID-41931241`, `PMID-40134905`

## DOM-004 — Field cues are calibration tools, not physiological measurements

### Supported claim

RPE, breathing, talk ability, recent race performance, and first-repetition response can help operationalize intensity when laboratory markers are unavailable. Their value is practical, but their exact correspondence with a physiological boundary is uncertain and affected by heat, fatigue, motivation, and workout duration.

### Training target

Combine pace ranges with effort and execution cues, especially where conditions or runner data make exact pace uncertain.

### Expected transfer

Better day-of control: runners can remain inside the intended session purpose instead of chasing a nominal pace that no longer represents the intended domain.

### Evidence type

This is an **inference** from measurement variability, environmental evidence, and the need for applied prescription. It is not a claim that RPE precisely measures CS or threshold.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate** for the need to preserve measurement uncertainty; **Low** for exact cue-to-domain mapping.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** operational relevance.

### What this does not prove

- that pace is unnecessary;
- that subjective effort should override all objective information;
- that the same RPE means the same physiology across runners.

### Sources

`PMID-37462761`, `PMID-41931241`, `PMID-25943653`, `PMID-34652333`

## Odyssey evidence boundary

Evidence supports using intensity domains to structure decisions. The following remain coaching judgments:

- exact pace ranges for Group A/B/C/D;
- whether a given threshold session sits nearer the middle or upper heavy domain;
- when a runner should change groups;
- how much pace is reduced for heat;
- whether recent race data are reliable enough to infer a boundary.

Pace groups should therefore be recalibrated by workout type and recent relevant performance rather than stored as permanent physiological classifications.
