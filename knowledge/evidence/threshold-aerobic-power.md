# Threshold, Tempo, and Aerobic-Power Training

Last reviewed: 2026-08-23

This file distinguishes sustained threshold-oriented work from severe-domain aerobic-power intervals. The categories can overlap in practice; the primary session purpose must be explicit.

---

## THR-001 — Threshold-oriented training targets sustainable high aerobic output

### Research question

What is the supported purpose of threshold/tempo training in distance runners?

### Supported claim

Threshold-oriented continuous running and medium/long intervals are established components of successful distance-running programs. They are used to accumulate work near an upper sustainable intensity without the rapid depletion associated with severe-domain running. Research supports the broad training quality, but threshold definitions and exact formats vary.

### Training target

- high but controlled aerobic output;
- pace/effort stability;
- ability to sustain a large fraction of aerobic capacity;
- tolerance of meaningful quality volume without repeated maximal efforts.

### Expected transfer

Potential improvements in threshold-related speed/power and the ability to maintain faster submaximal running. In longer events, this may support pace durability and lower relative cost at a given race pace.

### Acute evidence

A correctly controlled session should permit relatively stable repetitions or continuous work rather than progressive collapse. Blood lactate, ventilation, HR, and RPE responses depend on the exact threshold definition, duration, environment, and athlete.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Threshold work appears in successful multi-week programs and can improve threshold-related outcomes. Direct trials do not establish threshold-dominant training as universally superior to other balanced distributions.

### Performance evidence

Threshold-related speed is associated with endurance performance. However, the evidence does not identify one universally best continuous or interval format.

### Programming variables

- intensity anchor and definition;
- continuous versus intermittent format;
- work-bout duration;
- cumulative quality time;
- recovery duration/mode;
- environmental strain;
- training status and current durability.

### Population / context

Systematic reviews include trained to elite distance/endurance athletes. A 2024 meta-analysis directly comparing HIIT and continuous training for threshold outcomes contained only three studies and 72 athletes, with high heterogeneity and low certainty.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate** that threshold work is useful; **Low** for a unique optimal threshold dose or format.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** for a running Quality Session; **Moderate** for exact pace bands without individual threshold testing.

### Limitations

“Threshold” may refer to LT2, VT2, MLSS, CS-adjacent work, a race pace, or a coaching intensity zone. These are not identical.

### What this does not prove

- that threshold means one universal lactate value;
- that 20 minutes continuous, 3 × 8 minutes, or 2-km repetitions are interchangeable;
- that every threshold repetition should finish faster than it starts;
- that a threshold-heavy total program is optimal.

`PMID-34749417`, `PMID-35418513`, `PMID-38842374`, `PMID-41931241`

## THR-002 — Continuous tempo and cruise intervals solve different practical problems

### Research question

When should threshold-oriented work be continuous versus divided into repetitions?

### Supported claim

The literature supports threshold-zone work broadly but does not directly establish one universally superior format. Dividing work with short recoveries changes the acute response and can improve pace control or allow more accumulated quality; continuous work preserves uninterrupted duration and race-like rhythm.

### Training target

Choose the format that best preserves the desired combination of intensity, continuity, and quality volume.

### Expected transfer

- **Continuous tempo:** sustained rhythm, uninterrupted concentration, and duration-specific control.
- **Cruise intervals:** repeatable controlled work, group management, and potentially greater accumulated threshold-oriented volume with less local deterioration.

### Acute evidence

Recovery interrupts the metabolic and perceptual trajectory. The extent depends on recovery length/mode; a long recovery can change the session from continuous threshold stress into separated harder efforts.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Direct head-to-head evidence for common running formats is limited. Both appear in effective programs.

### Performance evidence

No strong evidence establishes one format as universally superior for race performance.

### Programming variables

- total work duration;
- rep length;
- recovery duration and jog/passive mode;
- pace variability;
- venue continuity;
- group spread and regrouping needs.

### Population / context

Indirect synthesis from distance-running reviews and interval-programming literature.

### Evidence strength

**Low-to-Moderate**.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** operational relevance.

### Limitations

The exact point at which cruise intervals become aerobic-power intervals depends on intensity, duration, and recovery—not the workout name.

### What this does not prove

- that short recovery automatically makes a session threshold training;
- that continuous tempo is always more race-specific;
- that one format must be used for all groups.

### Sources

`PMID-35418513`, `PMID-33826121`, `PMID-23539308`, `PMID-23832851`

## AP-001 — Multi-minute intervals are effective for accumulating high oxygen-uptake time

### Research question

Which work-bout characteristics tend to accumulate more time at or near VO2max during HIIT?

### Supported claim

A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of 86 articles and 239 protocols found that long work intervals (≥2 minutes) generally accumulated more time at or near VO2max than short (≤30 seconds) or intermediate intervals. Greater cumulative work duration was positively related to time ≥90-95% VO2max when longer intervals were used. An acute crossover in 12 highly trained middle-distance runners also favored 4 × 3 minutes over 24 × 30 seconds for time >90% VO2max in the tested protocols.

### Training target

Accumulate repeatable multi-minute work in the severe domain, creating a substantial high-aerobic-output stimulus.

### Expected transfer

Potential development of VO2max, maximal aerobic speed/velocity, and the aerobic support for faster race paces.

### Acute evidence

Strongest evidence concerns time at high oxygen uptake. Work duration and cumulative duration materially change this response. Variable-intensity intervals may increase time near VO2max in some protocols, but a newer network meta-analysis of 19 publications and 240 athletes reported substantial heterogeneity/inconsistency and no clear superiority of any protocol structure over even long intervals.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

HIIT improves aerobic-capacity/performance-related outcomes across many studies. The dose-response between acute time near VO2max and long-term adaptation remains incompletely established.

### Performance evidence

Interval-training interventions can improve time-trial performance. Response depends on training status, work duration, total work, intervention duration, and session count.

### Programming variables

- work bout ≥2 minutes versus shorter formats;
- cumulative quality duration;
- pace/intensity distribution within the rep;
- recovery length and mode;
- number of sessions/weeks;
- ability to preserve mechanics and target speed.

### Population / context

- Acute meta-analysis: mixed healthy/trained participants and exercise modalities.
- Network meta-analysis: 19 publications, 20 study units, 240 athletes; several protocol nodes were sparse and the network was heterogeneous/inconsistent.
- Runner-specific crossover: 12 highly trained middle-distance runners.
- Intervention meta-analysis: 491 adults, inactive through trained, mixed endurance modalities.

### Evidence strength

**High** for the acute multi-minute/high-oxygen-uptake relationship; **Moderate** for protocol ranking, chronic adaptation, and performance translation.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** for multi-minute running intervals; **Moderate** for exact dose because the evidence aggregates many protocols/modalities.

### Limitations

Time near VO2max is a proxy. Maximizing it is not the only objective and may increase fatigue or degrade mechanics. Exploratory protocol rankings should not be treated as proof of superiority.

### What this does not prove

- that every aerobic-power session must use ≥2-minute repetitions;
- that longer is always better;
- that 5 × 3 minutes is uniquely optimal;
- that short intervals have no role for speed, mechanics, or race specificity.

### Sources

`PMID-42237396`, `PMID-42482078`, `PMID-39835194`, `PMID-33826121`

## AP-002 — Acute VO2 stimulus, chronic adaptation, and race performance must remain separate

### Research question

Can an interval format that creates more time near VO2max be assumed to produce superior long-term adaptation or race performance?

### Supported claim

No. Acute time near VO2max indicates the physiological demand of a session but its dose-response relationship with chronic adaptation is not fully established. Multi-week interval programs improve VO2max and time-trial outcomes, but comparative results depend on athlete status and total program design.

### Training target

Use acute evidence to shape the desired session demand while checking fatigue cost, movement quality, and monthly progression.

### Expected transfer

Better alignment between workout purpose and likely adaptation without overclaiming the meaning of one physiological proxy.

### Acute evidence

Longer intervals generally increase accumulated high-oxygen-uptake time.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

HIIT can improve VO2max/MAP/MAV and other endurance determinants. More repetitions or greater total dose do not always produce greater adaptation.

### Performance evidence

Time-trial improvements occur, but trained athletes generally show smaller gains than less-trained participants and optimal dosing remains uncertain.

### Programming variables

- acute target versus monthly target;
- quality volume and fatigue;
- intervention dose outside Odyssey's control;
- training status;
- race specificity.

### Population / context

Mixed systematic reviews of acute protocols and multi-week interventions.

### Evidence strength

**High** for the need to separate outcome types; **Moderate** for the size of transfer to race performance.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** as a decision rule.

### What this does not prove

- that acute physiology is unimportant;
- that the session with the highest measured VO2 response is the best session in every phase;
- that a single Tuesday changes VO2max measurably.

### Sources

`PMID-42237396`, `PMID-33826121`, `PMID-41740126`, `PMID-42477971`

## AP-003 — HIIT and sprint-interval training are not substitutes

### Research question

Do HIIT and all-out sprint-interval training produce the same endurance adaptations?

### Supported claim

A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials and 460 participants found HIIT produced greater improvements in VO2max and MAP/MAV than SIT, although evidence certainty was mostly low/very low except for MAP/MAV. Sprint-oriented training can improve anaerobic and speed qualities, but it should not replace aerobic-power intervals when high aerobic output is the primary target.

### Training target

- **HIIT/aerobic power:** sustained severe-domain aerobic demand.
- **SIT/sprint:** maximal or near-maximal anaerobic/neuromuscular demand with different fatigue and recovery requirements.

### Expected transfer

Clearer selection between improving aerobic power and improving maximal/repeated speed qualities.

### Acute evidence

SIT produces very high power/speed and rapid fatigue; multi-minute HIIT accumulates more sustained oxygen-uptake demand.

### Chronic adaptation evidence

Both can improve fitness outcomes, but comparative evidence favors HIIT for aerobic-capacity markers.

### Performance evidence

HIIT showed greater TT improvement than SIT in some moderately trained subgroups, but certainty and protocol heterogeneity limit exact prescriptions.

### Programming variables

- maximal versus controlled intensity;
- rep duration;
- recovery completeness;
- technical quality;
- intervention frequency;
- total fatigue.

### Population / context

Mixed endurance/healthy participants in comparative trials; sprint-training reviews include many non-distance-running populations.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate**.

### Directness to Odyssey

**Moderate**.

### Limitations

The labels HIIT and SIT encompass heterogeneous protocols. Some short fast running used by runners is neither formal SIT nor maximal sprinting.

### What this does not prove

- that sprint work is unnecessary for 1-km performance;
- that every short repetition is SIT;
- that HIIT should be used every week.

### Sources

`PMID-41740126`, `PMID-36165995`, `PMID-38041768`

## AP-004 — Quality volume must be sufficient, but more is not automatically better

### Research question

How should cumulative interval dose be interpreted?

### Supported claim

Cumulative work duration influences acute high-oxygen-uptake time and multi-week interval response. However, intervention meta-analysis suggests increasing dose beyond minimum effective requirements may not continue to improve time-trial response, and sprint-repetition count does not clearly moderate VO2max gains.

### Training target

Accumulate enough quality work to create the intended stimulus while preserving rep consistency and acceptable residual fatigue.

### Expected transfer

More repeatable sessions and better monthly progression than prescribing maximal feasible volume each week.

### Programming variables

- total quality time/distance;
- number of reps/sets;
- pace decay;
- recovery;
- runner training status;
- time to race;
- 60-minute session constraint.

### Evidence strength

**Moderate**.

### Directness to Odyssey

**High** as a programming principle; **Low** for an exact minimum/maximum dose.

### What this does not prove

- that every runner should complete the same repetition count;
- that more high-intensity time is always harmful;
- that one session can be optimized independently from the rest of the week.

### Sources

`PMID-42237396`, `PMID-33826121`, `PMID-42477971`

## Applied distinction for PLAN design

| Primary purpose | Typical evidence-aligned emphasis | Common failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold / tempo | controlled sustained or repeated heavy-domain work; meaningful total duration | drifting into severe-domain racing and losing repeatability |
| Aerobic power | multi-minute severe-domain work with enough recovery to preserve quality | chasing maximal pace rather than sustained aerobic demand |
| 1-km speed endurance | distance/rhythm-specific fast work combining aerobic and anaerobic demands | calling every fast repetition “VO2” or making all reps all-out |
| Sharpening | low-volume intensity with high movement quality and large fatigue restraint | trying to create new fitness in the final days before racing |

The exact boundary between categories remains athlete- and protocol-dependent.
